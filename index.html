C. Theory

Acuity of hearing is measured with an audiometer. The human ear responds to vibrations from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

Hearing is affected by anything that interferes with conduction of sound waves to the cochlea, such as perforation of the tympanic membrane, middle-ear disease, cochlear disease, or problems in its connection with the central nervous system.

Types of audiometer

  • Pure tone audiometer: presents single-frequency tones at controlled levels to find air and bone conduction thresholds.
  • Speech audiometer: presents recorded or live speech at controlled levels to test speech recognition and the speech reception threshold.

Air conduction: transmission of sound through the external and middle ear to the inner ear. Bone conduction: transmission of sound to the inner ear through mechanical vibrations of the cranial bones and soft tissues.

Threshold search: the tone is first presented at a comfortable, clearly audible level, then the intensity is gradually reduced until the subject no longer perceives it; the lowest level still heard is the threshold.

Masking: when one ear is tested, sound can cross the skull to the other ear. Narrow-band white noise is fed to the non-test ear so that it cannot respond to the test tone.

F. Working principle

  • Tone generator / oscillator: produces pure tones, adjustable from about 250 Hz to 8 kHz.
  • Noise generator: produces white noise used for masking.
  • Equalization circuit: corrects the output so the level is calibrated across frequencies.
  • Attenuator: sets the intensity in dB HL, usually in 5 dB steps.
  • Power amplifier: drives the transducer.
  • Headphones: deliver air-conducted tones; the narrow-band filter shapes the noise sent to the masker.
  • Bone vibrator: placed on the mastoid to deliver bone-conducted tones.
  • Patient response button: lights the indicator when the patient hears the tone.

Why change frequency and intensity? Frequency tests different regions of the cochlea; intensity finds the lowest level the patient can hear at each frequency.

G. Audiogram

The audiogram plots frequency (Hz) on the X-axis and hearing level (dB HL) on the Y-axis, with louder levels lower on the chart. Right ear uses red, left ear uses blue. Air conduction: O (right), X (left). Bone conduction: < (right), > (left).

Thresholds between −10 and 25 dB HL (shaded) are normal. Higher values show hearing loss. If air and bone thresholds are close together, the loss is sensorineural; if air is worse than bone by more than about 10 dB, a conductive component is present.

These are simulated educational sample values, not a real patient’s diagnosis.

Procedure

Pure tone audiometer – air conduction

  1. Plug the headphones into the jack socket on the side panel.
  2. Switch ON the audiometer.
  3. Select RIGHT or LEFT depending on the ear being tested.
  4. Adjust the frequency dial step by step.
  5. Set the frequency and intensity so that the patient hears the sound.
  6. Gradually diminish the intensity with the dB level and note the threshold.
  7. Repeat the test to verify the reliability of the patient’s response.
  8. Repeat the test for frequencies below 1000 Hz as described in the laboratory procedure.
  9. Present the tone first at a higher level so the patient becomes aware of it, then reduce the intensity to the hearing threshold.

Pure tone bone conduction audiometry

  1. Plug the bone conduction vibrator into the appropriate socket.
  2. Position the bone switch to BC.
  3. Bone conduction can be tested up to 4000 Hz.
  4. Follow the same testing procedure as for air conduction.

In the simulator these steps map to: Connect headphones / bone vibrator, POWER, ear selector, AC/BC selector, frequency and dB controls, PRESENT TONE and RECORD THRESHOLD.
